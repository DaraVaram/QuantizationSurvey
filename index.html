Abstract

The deployment of quantized neural networks on resource-constrained edge devices, such as microcontrollers (MCUs), introduces fundamental challenges in balancing model performance, computational complexity, and memory constraints. Tiny Machine Learning (TinyML) addresses these issues by jointly advancing machine learning algorithms, hardware architectures, and software optimization techniques to enable deep neural network inference on embedded systems. This survey provides a hardware-oriented perspective on neural network quantization, systematically reviewing the quantization methods most relevant to MCUs and extreme-edge devices. Particular emphasis is placed on the critical trade-offs between model performance and the capabilities of MCU-class hardware, including memory hierarchies, numerical representations, and accelerator support. The survey further reviews contemporary MCU hardware platforms, including ARM-based and RISC-V-based designs, as well as MCUs integrating neural processing units (NPUs) for low-precision inference, together with the supporting software stacks. In addition, we analyze real-world deployments of quantized models on MCUs and consolidate the application domains in which such systems are used. Finally, we discuss open challenges and outline promising future directions toward scalable, energy-efficient, and sustainable AI deployment on edge devices.

1 Introduction

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have achieved state-of-the-art performance across various domains including image classification , speech recognition , and object detection . However, this exceptional performance comes at the cost of significantly increased computational and memory requirements . Modern DNN architectures often comprise millions or even billions of parameters, necessitating billions of multiply-and-accumulate (MAC) operations during inference. This workload places heavy demands on both compute and memory.

At the same time, the rapid growth of the Internet of Things (IoT) has significantly increased interest in resource-constrained embedded devices, particularly microcontroller units (MCUs). These MCUs are popular due to their low power requirements, high reliability, and ease of integration into diverse applications . Their integration with sensors, actuators, and networking capabilities enables sophisticated perception, intervention, and distributed intelligence at the edge.

Deploying complex DNNs on MCUs poses considerable challenges. Unlike graphics processing units (GPUs) or application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) accelerators, MCUs typically operate within strict constraints: limited on-chip memory (often just hundreds of kilobytes), minimal computational resources, low clock speeds (typically 40 to 400 MHz), and stringent power budgets (in the order of milliwatts) . Furthermore, real-time responses are often required , particularly in safety-critical applications such as healthcare devices , autonomous vehicles , and industrial robotics . In such scenarios, inference latency is critical, as delayed decisions can lead to severe consequences such as compromised safety, system failures, or worse.

This has led to the emergence of the tiny machine learning (TinyML) paradigm, which enables machine learning (ML) models to run on edge devices with extremely limited compute and memory resources, typically consuming only a few milliwatts or less . TinyML systems usually have memory footprints below 1 MB, often in the 64–256 KB range .

To meet the stringent resource constraints of MCUs, model compression has become essential for deploying DNNs on such platforms. These techniques reduce parameter size, computational cost, and memory usage, but often introduce accuracy–efficiency trade-offs. Common strategies include network pruning , low-rank decomposition , lightweight architecture design , neural architecture search (NAS) , and knowledge distillation . Among these, this survey focuses on network quantization, which preserves the model architecture while representing weights and/or activations using lower-precision formats.

Quantization exploits the inherent redundancy in neural networks, allowing models to retain high accuracy under reduced precision . It has therefore become a key enabler of TinyML and a widely adopted approach for efficiently accelerating inference, with potential benefits in energy and carbon footprint at scale. However, practical MCU deployment depends not only on reducing bit-width, but also on compatibility with the target platform’s numerical formats, operator coverage, memory organization and access constraints, software stack, and hardware execution path. This survey adopts this deployment-centered perspective by connecting quantization methods to MCU hardware, software stacks, and evidence from real-world deployments.

1.1 Existing Surveys

Recent surveys have addressed quantization, model compression, and TinyML deployment from different angles, yet these topics are often treated independently and remain limited in scope. The existing literature can be broadly grouped into three main lines: algorithm-centered quantization surveys , hardware-centered deployment surveys , and broader TinyML and edge-AI surveys .

Algorithm-centered surveys, such as those by Liu et al. and Gholami et al. , focus primarily on software-level advances in quantization, including low-bit quantization algorithms and training techniques across different applications and domains. Related works include Rokh et al. , who evaluate quantization methods for image classification, and Liu et al. , who discuss lightweight model design and hardware acceleration. Although extensive, these surveys give limited attention to hardware constraints and practical deployment concerns, particularly for MCUs and comparable edge-class devices.

Conversely, hardware-centered deployment surveys tend to emphasize platforms and architectures while giving less attention to quantization as a deployment methodology. For instance, Orășan et al. examine ARM Cortex-M-based MCUs with DNN deployment examples, but do not substantially explore quantization. Similarly, Liu et al. , Akkad et al. , and Giordano et al. analyze RISC-V, NPU-integrated, and low-power MCU platforms, but provide limited discussion of compression and quantization methods.

Broader TinyML and edge-AI surveys provide ecosystem-level overviews, but often lack depth on quantization as a deployment methodology. For example, Saha et al. cover MCU hardware and deployments, while Heydari and Mahmoud , Ray , and Somvanshi et al. focus more broadly on TinyML/TinyDL systems, applications, toolchains, and deployment challenges rather than quantization methodology as the central organizing theme.

Some studies partially bridge hardware and software aspects. For example, Capogrosso et al. survey model optimization and deployment frameworks, yet provide limited discussion of advanced quantization techniques. Lê et al. review efficient neural networks for TinyML on ultra-low-power MCUs, covering compression, deployment tools, and hardware constraints. Wang and Jia examine the broader edge-AI workflow, from data collection to hardware deployment. However, these works do not focus specifically on modern quantization strategies for MCU-class implementation.

Overall, existing surveys tend to emphasize algorithmic quantization, TinyML hardware, or application domains as separate topics. What remains missing is a deployment-centered synthesis that connects quantization methods with MCU hardware support, software toolchains, numerical representations, and evidence from real-world deployments.

2 Preliminaries

This section establishes the numerical and deployment concepts needed to understand quantization in the MCU setting. We proceed in three steps. Section 2.1 introduces the numerical formats that quantization moves between, and what each costs in memory, energy, and silicon. Section 2.2 formalizes quantization itself. Section 2.3 then works through the design choices that separate a quantizer which maps cleanly onto MCU hardware from one that does not.

2.1 Numerical Representations

Because MCU inference is executed by digital hardware with limited memory and fixed arithmetic resources, every weight, activation, and bias must ultimately be stored and processed using a finite number of bits. Since real numbers have infinite precision, their representation in hardware is necessarily an approximation. The process of encoding real numbers into a finite binary format is referred to as numerical representation . The choice of representation therefore directly affects precision, memory requirements, and computational efficiency; from a hardware perspective, it influences both the silicon area required for arithmetic units and the energy consumed during computation.

In broad terms, there exist two classes of numerical representations that are commonly used in DNN systems: (1) floating-point formats and (2) fixed-point formats. Floating-point formats are standard in general-purpose computing platforms like CPUs and GPUs, especially during training, where higher precision is needed. Fixed-point formats, on the other hand, are typically used in embedded systems such as MCUs and digital signal processors, where energy efficiency, silicon area, and memory constraints are critical.

The most common floating-point format is FP32 (floating-point 32-bit), also known as single or full precision . Each FP32 value occupies 32 bits (4 bytes) and follows the IEEE 754 standard . A number \(n\) in FP32 is represented as:

\begin{equation} n = (-1)^S \times (1 + m) \times 2^{(e - 127)}, \label{eq:fp32} \end{equation}

where \(S\) is the sign bit, \(e\) is the exponent (with a bias of 127), and \(m\) is the fractional part of the mantissa (also known as the significand), which is stored using 23 bits. The implicit leading 1 in the mantissa provides an effective precision of 24 bits. FP32 supports a wide dynamic range, approximately from \(10^{-38}\) to \(10^{38}\) (see Fig. 2, top), which typically exceeds the requirements for DNN inference .

However, this precision comes at a cost. It results in higher memory usage and requires more complex arithmetic hardware. Moreover, the increased complexity of FP32 arithmetic typically requires a dedicated floating-point unit (FPU) to perform these calculations. The high power consumption and silicon area of FPUs limit their suitability for low-power embedded systems .

On the other hand, fixed-point formats encode real numbers as scaled integers. A real number \(n\) in fixed-point format is typically represented as:

\begin{equation} n = \text{integer value} \times s, \label{eq:fixedpoint} \end{equation}

where \(\text{integer value}\) is a signed or unsigned integer stored in hardware, and \(s\) is a global scaling factor (power of two or arbitrary) that determines the position of the radix point. The separation between the integer and fractional parts is implicit and controlled by this scaling factor.

A major advantage of fixed-point formats is that they use simpler and more efficient integer arithmetic. Operations like addition, multiplication, and accumulation can be performed without the need for FPUs. As a result, fixed-point arithmetic is widely adopted in MCUs, digital signal processors, and low-power embedded platforms .

A commonly used fixed-point format is the INT8, which uses only 1 byte per value. Signed INT8 values range from \([-128, 127]\) (see Fig. 2, bottom left), while unsigned values range from \([0, 255]\) (see Fig. 2, bottom right). Although fixed-point representations offer less dynamic range and lower precision than floating-point formats, they provide substantial benefits in terms of memory savings, computational speed, and energy efficiency.

The dynamic range of a numerical format refers to the ratio between the largest and smallest values it can represent. Fixed-point formats have a narrower dynamic range, making them more suitable for data with limited variation, such as activations or weights in well-calibrated layers. Despite this limitation, fixed-point formats remain the preferred choice for efficient inference on edge devices due to their practical advantages.

The choice of numerical representation has a direct impact on computational efficiency, particularly in deep neural network workloads where MAC operations dominate both training and inference costs . Fixed-point arithmetic, especially INT8, offers substantial advantages over FP32 formats in this context. Relative to FP32, INT8 reduces storage by \(4\times\), which lowers memory bandwidth and cache pressure , and it lowers arithmetic cost as well, since integer units are simpler and cheaper to operate than their floating-point counterparts . These combined benefits make INT8 arithmetic highly effective for energy-efficient, low-latency inference in embedded and real-time systems, and make it the practical baseline for many MCU deployments.

In practical MCU deployment, the choice of numerical representation also determines whether computation can be expressed using simple integer MAC operations, whether tensors fit into limited on-chip memory, and whether the model can be mapped to the optimized libraries or NPU backends discussed later in Section 6, so the same nominal bit-width can carry very different practical value depending on the target hardware and runtime. This survey focuses on integer quantization because it remains the dominant path for MCU inference, but the representation space is broader. Alternative formats redistribute precision and dynamic range more effectively than standard fixed-point or floating-point, and although they are less central to practical MCU deployment than uniform integer arithmetic, they clarify what standard INT8 quantization trades away, and Section 5 summarizes them.

With this foundation in numerical formats and their implications for inference, we now formalize the quantization process as the transformation of high-precision real values into compact low-precision representations that are suitable for efficient hardware execution.

2.2 Formalization of Quantization

Consider a deep neural network (DNN) with \(L\) layers and learnable parameters \(\theta = \{W_1, W_2, ..., W_L\}\), applied to a supervised learning task. Each \(W_i\) denotes the weight tensor of the \(i^\text{th}\) layer. Given an input \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\), where \(d\) is the input dimensionality, the network computes predictions \(\hat{y} = f(\mathbf{x}; \theta)\) through a sequence of transformations. Assume that the model has been trained and the parameters \(\theta\) are stored in FP32 precision.

Quantization aims to reduce the numerical precision of both the model parameters and the intermediate activations, replacing floating-point values with low bit-width representations (e.g., INT8), while minimizing the loss in model performance (e.g., accuracy). This reduction is critical in scenarios constrained by memory, compute, or energy resources .

To enable quantization, we define a quantization function \(q(\cdot)\) that maps a real-valued input to a quantized integer. This can be generally written as:

\begin{equation} \mathbf{x}_{\text{int}} = q(\mathbf{x}; s, z) = \text{clamp} \left(\Big \lfloor \frac{\mathbf{x}}{s} \Big \rceil+ z~; q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}} \right), \label{eq:quant} \end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{x}\) is the original value, \(\mathbf{x}_{\text{int}}\) is the quantized integer. The scale \(s\) defines the quantization resolution of the quantizer and is typically computed as:

\begin{equation} s = \frac{\text{max}(\mathbf{x}) - \text{min}(\mathbf{x})}{2^b - 1}, \label{eq:scale} \end{equation}

where \(b\) is the bit-width of the integer representation (e.g., \(b = 8\) for INT8). The zero-point \(z\) (also called quantization bias) is an optional integer parameter that shifts the quantized range so that real zero is mapped exactly .

The rounding operation \(\left\lfloor \cdot \right\rceil\) maps the scaled input to the nearest integer and introduces quantization error. This operation is non-differentiable, which has important implications for training quantized models, since it motivates the training approximations used in QAT and quantized training, discussed in Sections 3 and 4.1.

The clamp\((\cdot)\) function restricts the result to lie within the target integer range \([q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}}]\), preventing overflow and underflow. The clamping operation is formally defined as:

\begin{equation} \text{clamp}(x, \alpha, \beta) = \begin{cases} \alpha & \text{if } x < \alpha, \\ x & \text{if } \alpha \leq x \leq \beta, \\ \beta & \text{if } x > \beta. \end{cases} \label{eq:clip_function} \end{equation}

This makes range selection and calibration central deployment decisions rather than only numerical details; they are the subject of Section 2.3.4.

To recover an approximate real-valued input from a quantized integer, a dequantization step is applied:

\begin{equation} \mathbf{x} \approx s \cdot (\mathbf{x}_{\text{int}} - z), \label{eq:dequantization_process} \end{equation}

enabling downstream computation using approximate values that can be efficiently processed in integer arithmetic units.

In sum, quantization seeks to determine the optimal quantization parameters, namely, the bit-width \(b\), scale \(s\), and zero-point \(z\) that balance model accuracy with efficiency gains in memory, computation, and energy. In this form, quantization can be understood as the mechanism that connects numerical representation to efficient embedded execution, replacing high-precision real-valued computation with lower-precision arithmetic that is better matched to MCU hardware and software stacks.

2.3 Quantization Design Choices for Deployment

Beyond bit-width alone, the practical effectiveness of quantization on MCUs depends on several design choices that determine both numerical fidelity and hardware efficiency. These choices are used throughout the survey to interpret why some methods map cleanly to MCU runtimes, while others remain mostly research despite strong compression results.

2.3.1 Uniform and Non-Uniform Quantization

The quantization method defined in \(\eqref{eq:quant}\) is known as uniform quantization . In this approach, the quantization levels are evenly spaced, meaning that each FP32 input is mapped to the nearest integer level using a fixed step size and rounding function \(\left\lfloor \cdot \right\rceil\). This results in a grid-like discretization of the input space, where values within the same quantization interval share the same quantized representation (see Fig. 3, left).

Uniform quantization is widely adopted due to its efficient implementation using fixed-point arithmetic , which enables fast and low-power deployment on resource-constrained hardware such as mobile and embedded devices. However, it is important to note that the distributions of weights and activations in neural networks are often non-uniform . As such, applying uniform quantization may lead to suboptimal allocation of representational capacity, especially in low bit-width regimes.

This motivates the use of non-uniform quantization, where the quantization levels are not evenly spaced (see Fig. 3, right). These methods adapt the step sizes to the data distribution, typically assigning more resolution to high-density regions (i.e., near zero) and less to low-density regions. A general formulation is given by:

\begin{equation} \mathbf{x}_{\text{int}} = q_{\text{non-uniform}}(\mathbf{x}) = X_i \quad \text{if } \mathbf{x} \in [\Delta_i, \Delta_{i+1}), \label{eq:nonuniform} \end{equation}

where \(\{ \Delta_i \}\) denotes variable-width partition intervals over the real-valued domain, and \(\{ X_i \} \subset \mathbb{Z}\) are the corresponding quantized integer levels. Unlike uniform quantization, neither the intervals \(\Delta_i\) nor the outputs \(X_i\) are required to be equidistant.

Common instantiations space the levels logarithmically, which concentrates resolution near zero and suits data with a wide dynamic range , or share a single centroid across a cluster of weights, an approach known as vector quantization or weight sharing .

Non-uniform quantization can therefore better match the distributions of weights or activations, especially at extremely low bit-widths, but it is generally less hardware-friendly and often requires more complex lookup or transformation logic . Consequently, although non-uniform methods remain important in the research literature, uniform quantization continues to dominate practical MCU deployment.

2.3.2 Symmetric and Asymmetric Quantization

An important design choice in uniform quantization is whether to employ a symmetric or asymmetric scheme. This affects how the scale \(s\) and zero-point \(z\) are computed in \(\eqref{eq:quant}\), and how well the quantization grid aligns with the data. The following paragraphs compare their formulations, use cases, and trade-offs.

Symmetric Quantization. In symmetric quantization, it is assumed that the values follow a distribution centered around zero, commonly a Gaussian distribution. As a result, the minimum and maximum values are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign. The zero-point is fixed at zero, which simplifies hardware implementation and computation. Symmetric quantization can be further categorized into symmetric signed (see Fig. 4, left) and symmetric unsigned quantization (see Fig. 4, center).

Symmetric Signed Quantization. In this setting, values span both negative and positive ranges, making it particularly suitable for weight tensors. The scale \(s\) is computed based on the maximum absolute value in the tensor:

\begin{equation} s = \frac{\text{max}(|\mathbf{x}|)}{127}, \label{eq:scale_symmetric_signed} \end{equation}

where the quantized range corresponds to an 8-bit signed integer: \([-128, 127]\). This ensures that the largest absolute value in the tensor \(\mathbf{x}\) maps directly to the maximum representable integer value of 127. Given that the zero-point \(z\) is fixed at zero, the dequantization formula simplifies to:

\begin{equation} \mathbf{x} \approx s \cdot \mathbf{x}_{\text{int}} . \label{eq:dequantization_symmetric_signed} \end{equation}

This method is efficient and particularly effective for data centered around zero, such as weights in neural networks. The fixed zero-point simplifies hardware implementation and is efficient for data with balanced positive and negative ranges. However, it may result in poor dynamic range utilization if the data is significantly skewed.

Symmetric Unsigned Quantization. Here, values are assumed to be strictly non-negative, with the zero-point still fixed at zero. This makes it ideal for activation tensors following ReLU operations. For an 8-bit unsigned integer range \([0, 255]\). The scale \(s\) is computed as:

\begin{equation} s = \frac{\text{max}(\mathbf{x})}{255} \label{eq:scale_symmetric_unsigned}. \end{equation}

This method is well-suited for skewed, one-tailed distributions, where values are strictly non-negative (i.e., ranging from 0 to a positive maximum), but unsuitable when values include negative components .

Asymmetric Quantization. Asymmetric quantization removes the assumption that the data is centered around zero, enabling more flexible mapping for skewed or unbalanced distributions. It introduces a non-zero zero-point \(z\), which ensures that the true zero in the real-valued range is exactly representable in the quantized domain (see Fig. 4, right). This is especially useful for activation tensors, which may span arbitrary ranges.

For an 8-bit representation (e.g., \([0, 255]\)), the scale and zero-point are computed as:

\begin{align} s &= \frac{\text{max}(\mathbf{x}) - \text{min}(\mathbf{x})}{255}, \label{eq:scale_asymmetric} \\ z &= \left\lfloor -1 \times \frac{\text{min}(\mathbf{x})}{s} \right\rceil . \label{eq:zero_point_asymmetric} \end{align}

Asymmetric quantization is ideal for activations and other data distributions that are not centered around zero, providing better accuracy for skewed or unbalanced data. The ability to adjust the zero-point introduces additional flexibility but also adds complexity to hardware implementations due to the variable zero-point.

In practice, many embedded deployments use symmetric quantization for weights and asymmetric quantization for activations, balancing hardware simplicity with representational flexibility. Non-negative activation ranges are also common after ReLU-like operators.

2.3.3 Static And Dynamic Quantization

Another important aspect of quantization design is when the clipping range \([q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}}]\) is determined. For weights, which remain fixed after training, this range is typically computed once and reused during inference. In contrast, activations vary with each input sample \(\mathbf{x}\), making the choice between static and dynamic quantization particularly relevant .

Static Quantization. Static quantization uses a fixed clipping range that is pre-computed, typically during a calibration phase, on a representative dataset. This approach avoids runtime overhead and is well-suited for deployment in latency-critical or resource-constrained environments. While it lacks adaptability to input variations, static quantization is widely adopted in practice due to its simplicity and efficiency.

The calibration process involves estimating activation ranges from sample inputs . Several methods have been proposed for determining optimal clipping thresholds (see Section 2.3.4), including minimizing the MSE between the original and quantized values . Other alternatives include entropy-based metrics and learning the clipping thresholds directly during training .

Dynamic Quantization. In dynamic quantization, the scale factor and zero-point are dynamically computed on-the-fly during inference using real-time statistics (e.g., min/max or percentiles) of the incoming activation values . This allows the quantizer to adapt precisely to the input distribution, often resulting in higher accuracy. However, the adaptivity comes at the cost of increased computational overhead due to the need to recompute quantization parameters for each input.

Thus, dynamic quantization adapts to each input by computing quantization parameters at runtime, achieving higher accuracy but at increased computational cost. Static quantization, on the other hand, fixes these parameters post-calibration, enabling efficient inference at the cost of some accuracy loss. For this reason, static quantization is generally preferred on MCUs, where latency, energy, and software simplicity are tightly constrained. For more adaptive strategies that bridge these trade-offs, see Section 4.3.2.

2.3.4 Calibration and Clipping Range Selection

As noted earlier, the range \([q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}}]\) represents the clipping range of the quantizer. Improper choice of this range can lead to clipping errors or large quantization errors. The process of determining appropriate clipping bounds is referred to as calibration. Below, we describe several commonly used calibration methods:

Min-Max Calibration. The most straightforward approach is to cover the full range of the input tensor \(\mathbf{x}\) by setting:

\begin{equation} q_{\text{min}} = \min(\mathbf{x}), \quad q_{\text{max}} = \max(\mathbf{x}). \label{eq:minmax} \end{equation}

While simple, this method is highly sensitive to outliers, which can disproportionately expand the dynamic range and lead to poor quantization fidelity .

Mean Squared Error (MSE). To mitigate the impact of outliers and reduce total quantization error, the clipping range can be selected to minimize the reconstruction error between the original and quantized-dequantized values. Specifically, the optimal range is defined as:

\begin{equation} (q_{\text{min}}^{*}, q_{\text{max}}^{*}) = \underset{q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}}}{\text{arg min}} \left\lVert \mathbf{x} - q^{-1}(q(\mathbf{x}; s, z); s, z) \right\rVert_{2}^{2}, \label{eq:mse_calib} \end{equation}

where \(q(\mathbf{x}; s, z)\) denotes the quantization function from \(\eqref{eq:quant}\), \(q^{-1}(\cdot; s, z)\) is the corresponding dequantization operation, and \(\left\lVert \cdot \right\rVert_{2}\) is the Euclidean or L2 norm.

Percentile-Based Calibration. In this approach, the quantization range is selected based on the empirical distribution of the dataset by selecting specific percentiles to define the lower and upper bounds of the range . For example, using the 99th percentile method, \(q_{\text{min}}\) is set to the 1st percentile and \(q_{\text{max}}\) to the 99th percentile of the dataset. Variants using 99.9th or 99.99th percentiles are also used in literature . This method effectively excludes extreme outliers and tends to yield more robust quantization results across layers. For a comprehensive comparison of calibration techniques and their effects on model accuracy and deployment, we refer interested readers to .

From an MCU perspective, calibration is especially important because tight precision budgets make poor range selection particularly costly.

2.3.5 Quantization Granularity

Quantization granularity refers to the level at which quantization parameters are applied within a neural network. This design choice directly impacts model accuracy, computational cost, and storage requirements. Coarser granularities simplify implementation but may compromise accuracy due to a less precise range of data across layer filters.

Global: Also referred to as network-wise quantization, this approach applies the same quantization parameters, namely a single shared scale and zero-point, uniformly across all weight tensors in the network , as illustrated in Fig. 5. By quantizing all layers using the same dynamic range, this method simplifies hardware implementation, reduces metadata storage, and minimizes computational complexity. However, such coarse granularity often results in significant accuracy degradation due to the large variation in value distributions across layers .

Beyond global quantization, finer-grained strategies are commonly used and can be classified into the following levels, arranged from coarse to fine granularity (see Fig. 6).

Per-tensor: Also known as per-layer, this method applies a single set of quantization parameters to the entire tensor, so all elements share the same clipping range . It is the most common choice for activations due to its simpler hardware implementation.

Per-channel: Also known as per-axis, this method assigns a distinct set of quantization parameters to each output channel, so every channel is scaled and clipped according to its own distribution. Compared to per-tensor (a single shared range), per-channel captures inter-channel variation, reducing quantization error and typically improving accuracy, while eliminating the need for global rescaling across channels. In practice, it is the standard choice for convolutional/linear weights, where channel-wise distributions often differ substantially.

Per-group: This method assigns a unique quantization parameter to each group of channels within a layer. It can be helpful when the distribution of the parameters within a layer varies significantly. For instance, it has been used in Transformer models (e.g., Q-BERT ) with fully connected attention blocks . However, this approach inevitably comes with the extra cost of accounting for different scaling factors. All in all, it offers a middle ground between per-tensor and per-channel.

Quantization granularity therefore spans from global to per-tensor, per-channel, and per-group. Moving to finer granularity generally improves accuracy by better matching local distributions, at the cost of extra quantization parameters and kernel complexity, and many CNN deployments settle on per-channel weights with per-tensor activations for that reason.

Taken together, the choices in this section show that quantization is less a single technique than a design space, and for MCU deployment the preferred operating point is usually not the most expressive quantizer, but the one that offers the best trade-off between numerical robustness, simplicity, and hardware support. We refer interested readers to for trade-offs associated with these design choices.

3 Primary Quantization Techniques

As for how quantization is applied, there generally exist two different approaches that are most commonly used: post-training quantization (PTQ) and quantization-aware training (QAT), as depicted in Fig. 7. These labels appear repeatedly in the deployment tables and application discussion, because they reflect different engineering costs before a model ever reaches the MCU.

3.1 Post-Training Quantization

PTQ algorithms, as the name suggests, are post-processing techniques that take pre-trained FP32 models and quantize their parameters, effectively converting them into a fixed-point network without the need for the original training pipeline . It is considered the simplest quantization technique, and is especially useful where data availability is limited, whether for privacy reasons or because the model is being prepared for edge deployment .

Depending on the availability of data, PTQ methods can be either data-driven or data-agnostic. Data-driven PTQ uses a small calibration set to estimate the appropriate quantization parameters, while data-agnostic approaches generate synthetic data or leverage model statistics to achieve calibration (see Section 4.6). Once calibration is performed, the quantized model can be used directly for inference without any further modification to its weights or structure.

As no further training is needed, this approach is straightforward and computationally inexpensive, making it an attractive choice for deployment. Once a model is quantized, it can be deployed to perform low-precision inference and associated computations, without any adjustments to the parameters. However, this convenience often comes at the cost of reduced accuracy. Quantization introduces numerical errors in parameters and activations (including the input), which propagate through subsequent layers. The accumulation of these errors can lead to misclassifications at the network output, producing an accuracy drop relative to the full precision model. As the bit-width decreases (e.g., INT8 or lower), these quantization errors tend to grow, increasing the likelihood of accuracy degradation, particularly in deeper or more sensitive models .

Despite this limitation, PTQ is widely used in practice and suffices for many TinyML and edge AI applications, where the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency is acceptable. For many MCU-oriented INT8 deployments, PTQ is a strong baseline.

3.2 Quantization-Aware Training

QAT addresses the limitations of PTQ by simulating quantization effects during training. Unlike PTQ, where quantization noise is introduced after training, QAT incorporates quantization directly into the forward pass, allowing the model to learn parameters that are more resilient to quantization errors .

This is achieved by inserting “fake” quantization operations into the network, which simulate low-precision inference-time computation, such as INT8 arithmetic. Specifically, both the inputs and weights are quantized to a fixed-point representation and then immediately dequantized back to floating-point. Although the underlying model parameters are stored and updated in FP32, this emulation allows the model to experience the quantization error that would occur during inference. As a result, the introduced error becomes part of the overall loss that the optimization algorithm, through the loss function and backpropagation, actively tries to minimize . The quantization emulation is applied only during the forward pass; the backward pass, including gradient computation and parameter updates, remains in full precision (FP32).

The backward pass must nonetheless overcome a critical challenge, because the quantization function, defined in \(\eqref{eq:quant}\), includes the non-differentiable rounding operation \(\lfloor \cdot \rceil\). This operation maps continuous values to discrete integers, preventing standard gradient-based optimization . To address this challenge, QAT typically employs the STE, which approximates the gradient of the quantization function by treating it as the identity function within the clipping range and assigning zero gradients outside of it. A quantizer whose gradient is zero almost everywhere therefore still permits gradient flow during backpropagation , as illustrated in Fig. 8. The gradient approximation is given by:

\begin{equation} \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{int}}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \approx \begin{cases} 1, & q_{\text{min}} s \leq \mathbf{x} + z \leq q_{\text{max}} s, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \label{eq:ste} \end{equation}

To better understand why QAT improves the robustness of quantized models, consider the example in Fig. 9, which visualizes the impact of quantization on the loss landscape. DNNs are trained by minimizing a loss function \(\mathcal{L}\), typically using stochastic gradient descent (SGD). During training, the gradient \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w}\) is computed with respect to each parameter \(w\), and the weights are updated iteratively in the direction of the negative gradient until the model converges to a local or global minimum.

On the one hand, Fig. 9(a) illustrates a one-dimensional loss curve for a model trained in full precision and later quantized using PTQ, so that nothing during training accounts for quantization and any deviation due to rounding arrives only after the model has converged. Suppose the parameter converges to \(w \approx -0.5\). Applying uniform quantization with scale factor \(s = 1\), this value is rounded to the nearest quantized level \(w_q = -1\), according to \(\eqref{eq:quant}\). This quantization step introduces a significant perturbation in the parameter value, resulting in a sharp increase in the loss. In this case, the model is said to have converged to a narrow minimum, where small deviations in weight values lead to large variations in the loss function .

On the other hand, Fig. 9(b) depicts training under QAT, where quantization noise is included as part of the training process from the outset. As a result, the model is guided toward flat or wide minima in the loss landscape, where small perturbations due to rounding have minimal impact on the loss value. These flatter regions provide better resilience to quantization, resulting in improved accuracy and generalization after deployment at low precision .

While QAT significantly improves robustness to quantization, the fake quantization operations it inserts in the forward pass add training overhead. A widely adopted strategy is therefore to apply QAT as a fine-tuning phase on top of a pre-trained FP32 model, which converges more quickly and achieves higher post-quantization accuracy than training from scratch under low-precision constraints . Quantization noise from the outset can impair the model’s ability to learn robust features, whereas a fully converged FP32 model provides a stable initialization from which the network adapts smoothly, making this two-stage pipeline especially attractive in resource-constrained environments.

For MCU deployment, PTQ and QAT present a practical trade-off, since PTQ is easier, cheaper, and faster to apply, whereas QAT is more computationally demanding but often necessary when aggressive compression or strict accuracy retention is required. Embedded pipelines therefore tend to start with PTQ and turn to QAT only when PTQ falls short.

4 Advanced Quantization Techniques

Beyond standard PTQ and QAT, recent research has introduced advanced quantization methods that aim to recover accuracy, reduce training or calibration cost, exploit hardware-specific precision support, or make tensor distributions easier to represent at low precision. While many MCU deployments today still rely primarily on INT8 PTQ and QAT, these advanced methods are included because they expose the algorithmic mechanisms, numerical trade-offs, and hardware limitations that may shape future MCU-class inference. Fig. 10 provides an overview of the methods discussed, categorizing them into major groups based on their core methodological innovations. Accordingly, each family is discussed not only in terms of compression potential, but also in terms of the practical barriers that currently limit adoption on MCU platforms.

4.1 Integer and Quantized Training

A notable extension of QAT is quantized training (QT), in which quantization is applied beyond inference simulation and into the training process itself, including parts of the backward pass and gradient computation. The goal is to reduce training cost and move closer to true end-to-end low-precision learning.

Several recent works illustrate this direction. Accurate Quantized Training (AQT) provides a practical software framework for low-precision training. Zhu et al. demonstrated full INT8 training for CNNs using gradient-stabilization techniques, showing that low-precision training can approach floating-point accuracy at lower cost. Octo reduces the overhead of conventional fake quantization, while Wang et al. proposed a gradient-distribution-aware INT8 framework to improve convergence. Collectively, these works show that quantized training is increasingly feasible, but still needs mechanisms to control gradient noise and instability.

Compared to PTQ and QAT for inference, QT remains considerably less mature for practical MCU deployment due to its optimization complexity and limited hardware and software support. Its main significance for MCU-class systems therefore lies in its longer-term potential, particularly as TinyML moves beyond fixed offline inference toward on-device adaptation, continual learning, and privacy-sensitive local updates. At present, however, integer and quantized training remain emerging research directions rather than standard practical deployment strategies for MCUs.

4.2 Extreme Low-Bit Quantization

While most practical MCU deployments rely on INT8 quantization, more aggressive schemes such as binarization and ternarization have been explored to further reduce memory and computational cost. In binarization, weights and/or activations are constrained to two values, typically \(\{-1,+1\}\) or \(\{0,1\}\), while ternarization introduces a third value, usually 0, yielding representations such as \(\{-1,0,+1\}\). These approaches can drastically reduce model size and replace conventional arithmetic with lightweight bitwise or sparse operations, making them attractive for severely resource-constrained settings.

Early work such as BinaryConnect and Binarized Neural Networks (BNNs) established the foundations of binary deep learning. XNOR-Net further improved the practical viability of binarization by introducing scaling factors to better approximate real-valued weights and activations, thereby reducing the accuracy loss of binary inference. Importantly, Embedded Binarized Neural Networks (eBNNs) demonstrated that binary networks can be adapted for low-power embedded platforms, highlighting the practical relevance of extreme low-bit inference beyond purely theoretical compression gains. In parallel, ternary approaches such as Ternary Weight Networks (TWNs) and Ternary-Binary Networks (TBNs) showed that introducing a zero state can provide a useful compromise between efficiency and representational capacity. A broader range of binarization and low-bit extensions has since been proposed to reduce the accuracy gap and improve training stability .

From an MCU perspective, however, extreme low-bit quantization remains a trade-off rather than a default choice. Although it can offer substantial theoretical gains in compression and arithmetic efficiency, the accuracy gap relative to INT8 and full precision often remains significant, especially for deeper networks and more complex tasks. In addition, many conventional embedded processors do not natively support 1- or 2-bit arithmetic, meaning that the expected gains may be reduced by packing, unpacking, and control overheads. Nevertheless, support for low-bit inference is gradually improving. For example, MCU platforms such as the MAX78000 and MAX78002 provide more direct support for low-bit neural network execution, illustrating how emerging hardware may increasingly enable practical deployment of extreme low-bit models . As such, extreme low-bit quantization remains an important research direction, particularly for future embedded systems that can better exploit its theoretical efficiency.

4.3 Mixed Precision Quantization

Rather than pushing all layers toward the same extreme low-bit setting, mixed precision quantization (MPQ) exploits the fact that different layers contribute unequally to quantization error, latency, and memory consumption. Instead of assigning a uniform bit-width across the entire network, mixed precision methods allocate different precisions to different layers, channels, or activations, thereby aiming for a better trade-off between compression and task performance . In this sense, mixed precision provides a more flexible alternative to aggressive uniform low-bit quantization, as sensitive layers can retain higher precision, while more robust layers can be assigned fewer bits.

The main challenge is determining the bit-width allocation itself, since the search space grows rapidly with model depth and architectural complexity. Broadly, existing methods address this problem in two ways: Rough Allocation, where bit-width allocation is based on partitions or heuristic metrics, and Adaptive Allocation, which emerges from learning-based or differentiable methods that dynamically adapt allocation through model training. A general overview of how mixed precision works is presented in Fig. 11.

4.3.1 Rough Allocation

Rough allocation methods typically assign bit-widths using heuristic rules or sensitivity estimates, often in post-training or search-based settings. A prominent example is Hessian-Aware Quantization (HAWQ) , which uses second-order information to identify layers that are more sensitive to quantization and therefore should retain higher precision. HAWQ-v2 extends this idea by using a richer characterization of layer sensitivity, while HTQ aims to estimate layer importance more efficiently using lower-cost approximations. Other criterion-based approaches formulate bit-width allocation as a constrained optimization problem, as in MPQCO , or learn layer-wise importance indicators, as in LIMPQ . More recently, InfoQ proposed a global information-flow criterion based on changes in mutual information at downstream layers, overcoming purely local sensitivity proxies.

In a more hardware-oriented direction, HAQ formulates bit-width selection as a hardware-aware search problem, assigning precision levels based on resource constraints such as latency, energy, and model size. Related methods such as CherryQ , OWQ , and REx similarly prioritize certain layers or weights based on importance or outlier behavior. Collectively, these methods show that even relatively simple sensitivity- or hardware-guided allocation can outperform uniform quantization by preserving precision where it matters.

4.3.2 Adaptive Allocation

Adaptive allocation methods, also referred to as learning-based approaches, instead learn bit-width assignments during training or optimization. In these methods, bit allocation is often treated as a policy search problem, where the objective is to find precision settings that best balance task performance with resource constraints such as memory, latency, or computational cost. Accordingly, the allocation is typically influenced by both task loss and deployment-related objectives.

For example, NIPQ incorporates hardware-related penalties directly into the loss, while MEBQAT and MetaMix aim to support multiple precision settings through meta-training. To better understand the structure of these frameworks, we refer readers to Fig. 12. Other methods, including CADyQ , CABM , and AdaBM , adapt bit-widths dynamically based on input content or lightweight calibration, and CoQuant uses teacher-student guidance to improve low-bit performance across multiple precision settings. Alternative formulations such as ALPS further generalize adaptive allocation to learned low-precision numerical formats. For a more comprehensive overview of adaptive quantization, we refer the reader to the survey by Rakka et al. .

Overall, MPQ is attractive for MCUs because it can recover part of the accuracy lost under aggressive low-bit quantization without fully sacrificing the efficiency gains of reduced precision. At the same time, its practical benefit depends strongly on hardware and runtime support, since variable bit-width execution introduces additional scheduling, kernel, and memory-management complexity. As such, mixed precision is best viewed as a structured compromise between uniform INT8 quantization and extreme low-bit deployment, more flexible than the former, yet often more practical than forcing the entire network into binary or ternary precision.

4.4 Hardware-Aware Quantization

MPQ already shows that different layers need not share the same precision. A natural next step is to make these precision choices explicitly aware of the target hardware. Although quantization is often motivated by reductions in latency and energy, its actual benefits depend strongly on the deployment platform. Speedups are not uniform across hardware, as they are influenced by factors such as memory bandwidth, cache hierarchy, on-chip memory capacity, and support for low-precision arithmetic. As a result, bit-width selection must be adapted not only to model sensitivity, but also to the constraints and capabilities of the target device.

This motivates hardware-aware quantization, in which bit-width configurations are selected based not only on accuracy and model size, but also on hardware-specific objectives such as latency, throughput, and energy. A major challenge here is the size of the search space. For an \(L\)-layer DNN, assigning one of \(S\) candidate bit-widths to each layer in a standard mixed-precision setting yields a search space of \(\mathcal{O}(S^L)\) . If weights and activations are quantized independently, this expands to \(\mathcal{O}(S^{2L})\). When targeting \(H\) different hardware platforms, the total search complexity grows further to \(\mathcal{O}(H \times S^{2L})\) . This makes exhaustive search impractical and motivates more efficient search strategies that are directly informed by hardware behavior.

A representative early example is HAQ , which uses reinforcement learning to automate per-layer bit-width selection for weights and activations using feedback from a hardware simulator. While HAQ demonstrated the value of hardware-guided allocation, its use of non-uniform quantization is less aligned with MCU deployment, where runtimes and kernels typically favor uniform quantization that can be implemented through integer-only arithmetic. To better reflect embedded constraints, Rusci et al. proposed a hardware-aware mixed-precision framework based on uniform quantization and integer-only execution, explicitly targeting memory- and compute-limited edge devices such as the STM32H7. Their results showed that hardware-aware mixed precision can outperform feasible uniform 8-bit models within the same memory budget.

Subsequent work has pushed hardware awareness further into the search process itself. HAWQ-v3 profiles actual per-layer hardware latency for different bit-widths and combines these measurements with Integer Linear Programming (ILP) to find quantization configurations under deployment constraints, moving beyond purely analytic cost estimates. OHQ goes further by performing the quantization search directly on edge hardware, thereby reducing the mismatch between offline search assumptions and real deployment behavior. Together, these works illustrate an important trend. As quantization moves closer to practical deployment, direct hardware feedback becomes increasingly important for finding meaningful precision configurations.

In practical MCU deployments, hardware-aware quantization is especially important because memory budgets, execution kernels, and supported numerical formats vary substantially across platforms. Accordingly, the best quantization strategy is often not the one that is theoretically most compressed, but the one that best aligns with the target hardware’s actual execution characteristics.

4.5 Redistribution

While hardware-aware quantization focuses on selecting bit-widths that best match the target platform, quantization performance also depends strongly on the distributions of weights and activations being quantized. In practice, accuracy degradation often arises because these distributions are non-uniform, heavy-tailed, or dominated by outliers, making them poorly matched to low-bit quantizers. This has motivated a complementary line of work based on redistribution, in which the statistical structure of weights or activations is modified to make low-precision representation more effective.

4.5.1 Distribution Uniformization

Uniform quantization is attractive for deployment because it maps cleanly to integer arithmetic, but it is often mismatched to the Gaussian-, Laplace-, or heavy-tailed distributions observed in neural network tensors . Distribution uniformization addresses this mismatch by spreading values more evenly across the available quantization range, as illustrated in Fig. 13. Representative methods include GDRQ , KURE , SqWQ , EQ-Net , and KurTail , which differ in whether they use group-wise adaptation, regularization, or reparameterization. At a high level, many such methods can be summarized as adding a redistribution term to the task objective:

\begin{equation} \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} + \lambda \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{redist}}, \label{eq:redist-general} \end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}\) is the original training loss, \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{redist}}\) encourages quantization-friendly tensor statistics, and \(\lambda\) controls the trade-off between task performance and redistribution strength.

4.5.2 Distribution Reshaping

A related direction is distribution reshaping, where the goal is not to make the distribution flat, but to better align values with quantization bins. Bin Regularization (BR) reduces within-bin dispersion, while Range Restriction Loss (R2) compresses the global range before PTQ or QAT. Both can be viewed through \(\eqref{eq:redist-general}\), with \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{redist}}\) instantiated as either a bin-concentration or range-restriction objective.

4.5.3 Outlier Redistribution

Another major source of quantization error is the presence of outliers, whose large magnitudes can dominate range selection and reduce the resolution available for the majority of values. Outlier redistribution methods therefore aim to suppress, smooth, or spread extreme values so that the effective dynamic range becomes easier to quantize.

Representative approaches include channel-wise smoothing, as in SmoothQuant , OS+ , OmniQuant , and FQ-ViT ; orthogonal or rotation-based spreading, as in Hadamard-based methods and DuQuant ; and magnitude-oriented suppression, as in AdderQuant and MagR . Despite their different mechanisms, the common purpose is to prevent a small set of extreme values from determining the entire quantization range.

4.5.4 Bias Redistribution

Bias redistribution methods instead translate the distribution so that values align more favorably with quantization intervals. QuantSR , NoisyQuant , and RAOQ illustrate this family through learnable shifts, fixed noise-based biasing, or recentering operations. Compared with scaling- or rotation-based methods, these approaches emphasize additive alignment rather than contraction or spreading alone.

Overall, redistribution methods are best understood as complementary to bit-width selection and hardware-aware search. Whereas mixed precision and hardware-aware quantization decide how many bits to allocate and where, redistribution methods instead reshape the underlying data so that those bits can be used more effectively. This makes them particularly relevant in aggressive low-bit regimes, where the mismatch between tensor statistics and quantizer structure becomes a dominant source of performance loss.

4.6 Data-Agnostic Quantization

Both QAT and PTQ typically rely on calibration, where representative data are passed through the model to estimate activation ranges, scales, zero-points, and clipping thresholds. This assumption is problematic when calibration data are unavailable, privacy-sensitive, or poorly representative of future edge inputs. Data-agnostic quantization addresses this setting by estimating or correcting quantization error from the model itself rather than from external calibration data. Fig. 14 contrasts this setting with conventional calibrated PTQ.

These methods can be grouped by the source of information they use to replace calibration. One family relies on internal statistics and analytic correction. Data-Free Quantization (DFQ) , for example, combines cross-layer range equalization, bias absorption, and bias correction to reduce outliers and compensate output drift without a representative dataset. A second family formulates data-agnostic quantization as local error control over the weights. SQuant uses layer-wise Hessian approximations to guide discrete optimization, while REx represents weights through accumulated low-bit residuals, linking data-agnostic quantization with mixed-precision reconstruction.

A third family changes the quantization parameterization or redistributes lost information after compression. PNMQ searches non-uniform mixed-precision grids using only model weights and then applies DFQ-style correction . UDFC instead compensates for damaged or coarsely quantized channels by redistributing their contribution through recoverable full-precision channels. Together, these approaches show that calibration data can be replaced by a combination of weight statistics, analytic correction, residual reconstruction, and channel-level recoverability.

For MCU deployment, data-agnostic quantization is most useful when the model must be compressed after training but representative data cannot be stored, shared, or collected on-device. Its main limitation is that it cannot fully capture input-dependent activation behavior, so it is generally best viewed as a fallback or privacy-preserving alternative to calibrated PTQ rather than a universal replacement. For a more comprehensive overview of data-agnostic quantization, we refer the reader to the survey by Kim et al. .

5 Alternative Numerical Representations

Most MCU-oriented quantization pipelines rely on fixed-point or integer arithmetic because these formats are simple, efficient, and widely supported by embedded hardware. Their limited dynamic range and expressivity nevertheless leave room for alternative numerical representations that redistribute precision and dynamic range differently from conventional INT8 inference. Reduced floating-point, adaptive fixed-point, and tapered-precision formats are reviewed here on that basis, with Fig. 15 grouping them by how each makes the trade. Whether any of them is practical still depends on hardware support, runtime integration, and optimized kernels, so reviewing them also shows what future MCU platforms would need in order to move beyond standard uniform affine integer quantization.

5.1 Reduced Floating-Point Formats

One direction revisits floating-point design. The sign bit, exponent, and mantissa structure introduced in Section 2.1 can be written in a general form as:

\begin{equation} n = \text{sign} \times 2^{\text{exponent value}} \times \text{mantissa value}. \label{eq:fp-general} \end{equation}

This form generalizes \eqref{eq:fp32}, the IEEE 754 single-precision case. The dynamic range FP32 offers often exceeds what is needed in DNNs, particularly during inference, leading to low information-per-bit density and unnecessary costs in power, area, and latency . The formats reviewed below therefore specialize the same general form differently, dividing the available bits between the exponent and the mantissa in ways better matched to inference.

FP16 uses a 5-bit exponent and a 10-bit mantissa, while Google’s BFloat16 (BF16) retains the 8-bit exponent of FP32 but reduces the mantissa to 7 bits. NVIDIA’s TensorFloat-32 (TF32) also uses 8 exponent bits but limits the mantissa to 10 bits, resulting in a 19-bit format. These formats preserve the dynamic range required for DNN workloads while reducing bit-width, leading to faster computation and improved memory efficiency, and they are now widely adopted across training accelerators, including those from Google, NVIDIA, and Intel.

Researchers have also explored the theoretical foundations of FP bit allocation. For an FP format with a total bit-width of \(N\), the bit-lengths of the mantissa (\(n_1\)) and exponent (\(n_2\)) can be optimized by analyzing propagation of quantization error. With one bit reserved for the sign, the relationship becomes \(n_1 = N - n_2 - 1\), and by differentiating the quantization error function and setting the result to zero, the optimal split can be derived by minimizing expected error through analytical means . TF32 follows such a rationale.

More aggressive reductions have targeted inference use cases, including 8-bit floating-point (FP8) formats , which show that with proper QAT, competitive accuracy can be achieved. In practice, such formats are typically used for storing weights and activations, while higher precision (e.g., FP32) is retained for accumulations and weight updates to avoid numerical degradation .

Other reduced-floating-point variants improve compression or adaptability. Microsoft’s MSFP shares a single exponent across a group of values, known as a “bounding box”, allowing smaller per-value mantissas and enabling Single Instruction Multiple Data (SIMD)-style acceleration. Block and Subword-Scaling Floating-Point (BSFP) builds upon this by representing each full precision weight as a combination of two low-bit sub-word vectors, each scaled independently by low-bit floating-point factors, capturing both coarse and fine variations in weight distributions. Together with Tesla’s CFloat8/CFloat16 , these formats show how major hardware vendors tailor numerical formats to inference and training workloads .

Other adaptive formats focus on structural optimizations. AdaptiveFloat dynamically sets the exponent bias per tensor to minimize quantization error, while the adaptive numerical data type (ANT) infers the exponent-mantissa boundary from the bitstream itself, based on the first occurrence of 1. Dynamic-Length Float (DFloat) identifies redundancy in BF16’s exponent representation and applies Huffman coding to compress it losslessly, yielding highly compact encodings (e.g., 11-bit representations for large-language-model weights). These formats are especially relevant for accelerators and training-oriented hardware, but they remain less common in MCU deployment, where integer kernels and memory-constrained runtimes are dominant.

5.2 Adaptive Fixed-Point Formats

A second direction extends fixed-point quantization itself. Conventional low-precision fixed-point suffers from limited dynamic range and bias around zero, making it best suited for narrow-support distributions and often requiring careful calibration to avoid overflow or underflow. To overcome these limitations, several alternative fixed-point schemes have been proposed that better align numerical resolution with the statistics of the target data.

A notable instantiation of \eqref{eq:fixedpoint} is TFLite’s Zero-Skew format , a uniform fixed-point representation with zero-centered bins. In this scheme, a real number \(n\) is represented as:

\begin{equation} n = s\,(n_{\text{int}} - z), \label{eq:zero-skew} \end{equation}

where \(n_{\text{int}}\) is the stored integer, \(s\) is the skew (scale factor), and \(z\) is the zero-point. Here \(s\) and \(z\) are the same quantization parameters defined in Section 2.2. This zero alignment shifts the quantization grid to match the distribution mean, reducing bias and improving accuracy for asymmetric data.

Beyond grid alignment, adaptability in fractional precision offers further gains. F8Net maintains a fixed bit-width but determines the position of the fractional boundary separately for each layer based on the statistical properties of its activations or weights. By analyzing the standard deviation of the target tensor, F8Net selects a precision setting that ensures the representable range is well matched to the layer’s value distribution. This per-layer adjustment minimizes saturation and underutilization of the numeric range, thereby reducing quantization error while preserving compatibility with efficient integer computation.

Complementary to per-layer adaptation, VS-Quant increases scale granularity through a hierarchical design that combines coarse per-channel scaling with lightweight per-vector factors. This finer partitioning locally reduces dynamic range, improving low bit-width accuracy, particularly in PTQ, while keeping metadata and computation overhead minimal. The vector-level granularity aligns with typical accelerator MAC tile sizes, preserving efficient fixed-point execution.

These approaches retain much of the efficiency of integer arithmetic while improving how the available numeric range is allocated across layers, channels, or vectors. They are therefore closer to MCU-oriented quantization than entirely new number systems, although their value still depends on runtime and kernel support.

5.3 Tapered-Precision Formats

A third direction explores tapered-precision formats, which vary the number of mantissa bits with the magnitude of the value being encoded rather than fixing the field widths in advance. Two are relevant here: (1) Posit, also known as Type III Universal Number (Unum) , and (2) takum .

On the one hand, Posit offers superior dynamic range and precision with the same or fewer bits than traditional floating-point . Posit numbers are parameterized by \((N, es)\), where \(N\) is the total bit-width and \(es\) is the number of exponent bits, although the 2022 posit standard fixes \(es\) at 2 and parameterizes a posit by its bit-width alone . Internally, a Posit number is composed of four fields: sign bit, variable-length regime, exponent (of fixed length \(es\)), and a variable-length mantissa. The regime field enables Posit’s tapered precision, allocating more precision to values close to 1 and less to extreme values , which suits deep neural networks, where parameters and activations typically follow zero-centered Gaussian-like distributions .

A real number \(n\) is represented in the Posit format by \(\hat{n}\) as follows:

\begin{equation} \hat{n} = (-1)^S \times u^k \times 2^e \times (1 + m), \label{eq:posit} \end{equation}

where \(S\) is the sign bit, \(e\) the exponent, \(m \in [0, 1)\) the mantissa fraction as in \eqref{eq:fp32}, \(k\) the regime value, set by the length and polarity of the regime run, and \(u = 2^{2^{es}}\) the so-called useed.

This tapered-precision behavior makes Posit attractive for low-precision DNN inference from a numerical perspective, and Posit arithmetic has been shown to run effectively on MCUs with minimal accuracy loss and is also being explored for approximate computing at the edge . Posit coprocessors have since been integrated into microcontroller-class RISC-V systems on chip, where a posit unit is reported to be both smaller and lower-power than an equivalent single-precision FPU . Nevertheless, no commercial MCU ships a posit unit , and the embedded inference runtimes surveyed in Section 6.2 remain integer-centric.

Takum , on the other hand, keeps this variable mantissa length but replaces the mechanism that encodes the exponent. Where a Posit spends a unary run of bits on the regime, bounded only by the word size, a takum uses a one-bit direction flag and a fixed three-bit regime that states how many characteristic bits follow. The prefix is therefore never longer than 12 bits, so an \(N\)-bit takum always retains at least \(N - 12\) mantissa bits, whereas a Posit can in principle be left with none. That floor bounds the relative approximation error, which for a Posit is unbounded from below and so resists the standard numerical-analysis techniques developed for floating-point. Both combine the same three ingredients, a sign, a scale that varies with magnitude, and a mantissa, but assemble them differently. Where a Posit multiplies a linear significand by powers of the useed and of two, as in \eqref{eq:posit}, a takum raises a single base to a logarithmic value \(\ell\), so that a real number \(n\) is represented in the takum format by:

\begin{equation} \hat{n} = (-1)^{S} \sqrt{\mathrm{e}}^{\,\ell}, \qquad \ell = (-1)^{S}(c + m), \label{eq:takum} \end{equation}

where \(S\) is again the sign bit, \(c\) the characteristic, an integer whose sign and width follow from the direction and regime bits, and \(m\) the mantissa fraction as in \eqref{eq:posit}. The logarithmic value \(\ell\) is therefore confined to \((-255, 255)\).

This bounds the format in a way Posit is not. A takum reaches its full dynamic range at \(N = 12\) and holds it constant thereafter, spanning roughly \(4.2 \times 10^{-56}\) to \(2.4 \times 10^{55}\) in the standard logarithmic variant, so every additional bit buys precision rather than range . It bounds the decoder too. With the regime confined to eight states, parsing reduces to a 16-byte lookup table (LUT) shared across all precisions, whereas a Posit regime may span the whole word and needs bit-counting logic on every input bit. Implemented on a field-programmable gate array (FPGA), an open-source codec reports lower latency and fewer LUTs than a comparable Posit codec on that basis .

For MCU deployment, however, takum is at an earlier stage than Posit. Its published evaluations are in numerical linear algebra rather than neural networks, covering sparse iterative solvers , eigenvalue computation with the implicitly restarted Arnoldi method , spectral methods via the FFT , and integer representability across the three formats . No takum ASIC or instruction set extension has been reported either, although one case study examines restructuring a SIMD instruction set around the format . The format also matches or outperforms Posit and BFloat16 in all reported scenarios except addition and subtraction , a consequence of the logarithmic significand that bears directly on the accumulate-heavy kernels of DNN inference, and the posit literature notes that its bounded range leaves it with a more limited tapered behavior than a Posit . A balanced-ternary sibling, tekum , carries the same tapered-precision idea into ternary logic, but remains an analytical design with no hardware or software implementation.

Posit and takum therefore trade differently against the same constraint, the former concentrating precision near unity at the cost of an unbounded regime and no worst-case mantissa guarantee, the latter bounding both at the cost of dynamic range and of the accumulate-heavy operations that dominate inference.

Overall, alternative numerical representations broaden the design space beyond standard integer quantization. For practical MCU inference, however, uniform and affine integer quantization remain the most widely adopted choices because they align best with existing kernels, runtimes, and hardware support. For a more comprehensive overview of emerging number systems, we refer the reader to the survey by Alsuhli et al. , for Posit in particular to the survey by Mallasén et al. , and for hardware-oriented approximate arithmetic to the study by Guntoro et al. .

6 Hardware and Software Deployment Stack

Deploying quantized neural networks on MCU-class devices is a full-stack problem. The practical value of a quantization method depends not only on the numerical precision of the trained model, but also on the arithmetic supported by the target processor, the available memory hierarchy, the operator kernels, the compiler/runtime, and the degree of integration between the software flow and the hardware backend. Rather than treating hardware and software as independent topics, this section reviews their interaction in MCU deployment, first outlining the main hardware families used for quantized inference and then examining the software frameworks and toolchains that map models onto them.

6.1 Hardware Platforms

At the hardware level, MCU deployment targets can be grouped into three main families, namely general-purpose ARM Cortex-M MCUs, RISC-V-based MCUs, and NPU-integrated MCUs augmented with dedicated neural processing units (NPUs). These families differ not only in compute capability, but also in the maturity and flexibility of the software ecosystems built around them. 6.1.1 ARM Cortex-M MCUs

ARM Cortex-M devices , particularly Cortex-M4, remain one of the most common baselines for TinyML deployment because they combine low cost, broad availability, and low-power operation . Billions of MCUs are built around this family , and they can operate for long periods under tight energy budgets . This makes them a natural reference point for judging extreme-edge deployability.

Architecturally, the Cortex-M4 is a 32-bit reduced instruction set computer (RISC) processor based on the ARMv7E-M instruction set architecture (ISA), with DSP-oriented support for efficient integer arithmetic and MAC operations. Some variants, such as Cortex-M4F and Cortex-M7, include floating-point support, but integer execution remains more relevant for TinyML because it reduces memory footprint and avoids the cost of floating-point arithmetic on devices with limited compute and memory.

From a quantization perspective, the main strength of Cortex-M platforms is their suitability for conventional fixed-point inference, especially INT8. Lower-precision arithmetic can reduce memory traffic and storage requirements , but most Cortex-M MCUs lack native hardware support for dynamic mixed-precision or arbitrary sub-byte tensor operations. As a result, sub-8-bit and mixed-precision deployment on this family usually depends on packing strategies and specialized kernels rather than direct hardware support . Thus, ARM Cortex-M devices are best viewed as the most reproducible and accessible TinyML baseline, especially when the goal is stable deployment rather than hardware-level quantization exploration.

6.1.2 RISC-V-based MCUs

RISC-V-based MCUs have gained attention as a flexible alternative to ARM-based MCUs, whose proprietary and relatively fixed instruction sets can limit specialization for edge AI workloads . The open and extensible nature of RISC-V makes it particularly attractive for hardware/software co-design, since low-bit arithmetic, packed SIMD instructions, dot-product units, and mixed-precision support can be introduced closer to the ISA and microarchitecture level .

Several RISC-V cores and platforms have shaped this design space. Representative cores include RV12 , E203 , RI5CY , and Rocket . A major research platform is PULP , which integrates low-power RISC-V cores and has become a foundation for many embedded machine learning accelerators. Commercial derivatives such as GAP8 and GAP9 extend this direction by combining clustered RISC-V cores with on-chip memory and an integrated neural accelerator for efficient edge inference, and are discussed with the NPU-integrated devices in Section 6.1.3.

From a quantization perspective, the main advantage of RISC-V is that precision support can be treated as part of the hardware design rather than only as a software workaround. For example, Ottavi et al. proposed a mixed-precision inference core and controller around a RISC-V processor, while Dustin and DARKSIDE further explore mixed-precision SIMD and reconfigurable execution for low-power learning and inference. These designs show that, with careful ISA and microarchitectural co-optimization, RISC-V systems can approach accelerator-like efficiency while preserving programmability.

However, the RISC-V ecosystem is still less standardized than the ARM Cortex-M ecosystem. Many advanced low-bit and mixed-precision designs remain research prototypes or FPGA-based demonstrations, while commercially available RISC-V MCUs such as ESP32-C3 , ESP32-C6 , and ESP32-P4 are often positioned as general-purpose or wireless IoT MCUs rather than dedicated neural inference platforms. Thus, RISC-V is best viewed as a promising and flexible foundation for quantized inference, especially when the goal involves hardware-aware precision design.

For broader coverage of RISC-V architectures in embedded ML, we refer the reader to the surveys by Akkad et al. and Liu et al. .

6.1.3 NPU-Integrated MCUs

In recent years, NPU-integrated MCU platforms that combine traditional CPUs with dedicated NPUs have emerged as a promising class of devices for ultra-low-power edge AI. These MCU-scale NPUs, often referred to as micro-NPUs (\(\mu\)NPUs) , help fill the gap between power-hungry GPUs and standalone MCUs that lack sufficient compute capacity for real-time neural inference . By tightly integrating acceleration into MCU-class devices, NPUs target operations such as convolution and matrix multiplication while preserving low-power operation .

Since CNNs remain among the most common neural architectures deployed at the edge, many NPUs are designed primarily as CNN accelerators and rely on quantization, pruning, and related compression methods to meet memory, latency, and energy constraints .

Representative NPU-integrated MCU platforms differ mainly in how the accelerator is coupled to the host cores. The MAX7800x family illustrates a tightly coupled design, combining MCU control cores with a dedicated CNN accelerator that supports low-bit weights. The GAP8 and GAP9 platforms pair clustered low-power RISC-V cores with on-chip memory and dedicated hardware convolution engines, the HWCE and NE16, respectively. A third path attaches a dedicated neural backend to an MCU-class processor, as in Cortex-M55 devices with Ethos-U accelerators such as the Himax HX6538-WE2 and the STM32N6 with its Neural-ART accelerator.

For quantized deployment at the extreme edge, the common advantage of these platforms is that they move the main neural-network workload beyond CPU-only execution while remaining within tight power budgets. Dedicated accelerators can exploit low-precision arithmetic to speed up convolution, matrix multiplication, or other supported neural operators. However, this shifts the deployment challenge from general CPU-level optimization to accelerator-aware mapping, where the model must fit the device’s supported operators, precision formats, memory layout, SRAM limits, and scheduling constraints. Overall, NPU-integrated MCUs mark a shift from CPU-bound inference toward low-power, low-latency neural deployment on severely constrained devices. For a broader overview of micro-NPU architectures and benchmarks, see Millar et al. .

6.2 Software Frameworks and Deployment Tools

The software side of MCU deployment can be understood through three main layers. The first is the model-development front end, where frameworks such as TensorFlow/LiteRT , PyTorch , and ONNX are commonly used for training, conversion, or model exchange. The second is the MCU inference runtime, where the model is executed under strict memory and operator constraints. The third is the hardware-specific code-generation flow, which determines how the network is mapped to kernels, memory regions, and accelerator-specific execution units.

For ARM Cortex-M deployment, TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers (TFLM) and CMSIS-NN form the most common software stack. TFLM provides a lightweight inference runtime with static memory allocation, while CMSIS-NN provides optimized kernels for common quantized neural network operators. STM32Cube.AI offers a more vendor-integrated path for STM32 devices by converting trained models into deployable embedded C code. Edge Impulse is also widely used for end-to-end TinyML prototyping, especially when data collection, feature extraction, training, and firmware generation are handled within one workflow. These tools make deployment accessible and reproducible, but they mostly favor standard INT8 inference rather than sub-byte or dynamic mixed-precision modes.

For commercial RISC-V MCUs, deployment largely follows general-purpose runtimes and optimized software kernels. Ariel-ML , for example, combines embedded Rust, Ariel OS, and IREE to deploy INT8 models across heterogeneous MCU families, including the ESP32-C3, while exposing the latency and memory cost of the compiler and runtime itself. On open research targets, PULP-NN and XpulpNN show how low-bit inference benefits from co-designed kernels and ISA-level extensions, and Kaneko and Meng similarly combine integer PTQ, buffer optimization, and SIMD kernels on the CV32E40p CPU path.

For accelerator-augmented MCUs, deployment is usually dominated by vendor-specific toolchains. The MAX78000 and MAX78002 platforms use ai8x-training and ai8x-synthesis to train, quantize, map, and synthesize models for the CNN accelerator. The GAP8 and GAP9 platforms use the GAPFlow toolchain, including nntool and the Autotiler , for graph transformation, quantization, memory tiling, DMA orchestration, and code generation across their clustered cores and convolution engines. Ethos-U-based devices use the Arm Ethos-U toolchain and runtime support to map compatible networks to the microNPU. STM32N6 follows a similar accelerator-oriented deployment model through ST’s Neural-ART software stack. These flows can provide strong latency and energy results when the model fits the accelerator, but they reduce portability because the network must satisfy device-specific operator, memory, and scheduling constraints.

Other frameworks such as TinyMaix , NNoM , TinyNN , MinUn , HEEPstor , and ExecuTorch are also relevant in specific research or deployment settings. They extend this landscape with minimalist C runtimes, PyTorch-to-TFLM conversion, mixed-precision memory management, hardware/software co-design flows, and emerging PyTorch-edge deployment paths. More broadly, these tools highlight the importance of exporter and compiler co-design, where model export, quantization parameters, operator coverage, memory planning, and backend code generation are aligned with supported kernels, memory layouts, and hardware execution paths. This reinforces the main point that quantization should not be evaluated only as a training-side technique, since its deployment value depends on whether the target stack can properly exploit the chosen precision.

7 Applications

The preceding sections showed that quantized MCU deployment is not determined by model compression alone, but by the interaction between quantization method, hardware capabilities, and deployment software. The application literature makes this especially clear. In practice, real deployments reflect not only what is theoretically possible under low precision, but also what current runtimes, kernels, memory systems, and toolchains can reliably support on-device.

Accordingly, this section reviews representative real-world deployments through the three MCU families discussed in Section 6.1: ARM-based, RISC-V-based, and NPU-integrated. This organization is retained intentionally to preserve the survey’s hardware-oriented perspective. At the same time, our discussion emphasizes the cross-case patterns that recur across domains, particularly with respect to latency, memory footprint, energy consumption, and the practical limits of different quantization strategies. Fig. 16 summarizes this path from model optimization and quantization to hardware-aware conversion and MCU execution.

To keep this review consistent despite differences in tasks, metrics, and reporting practices, we follow the selection and reporting principles below:

  1. Only genuine MCU deployments are considered. We therefore exclude studies that target higher-end embedded platforms, such as Raspberry Pi boards, even when such systems are often mischaracterized as MCU-class devices. This keeps the reviewed works aligned with the strict memory, compute, and power constraints that define MCU-class deployment.
  2. Since task-performance metrics vary across application domains, we report the metric used in each study, including accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, precision, mean average precision (mAP), area under the curve (AUC), or success rate.
  3. In cases where multiple datasets or model variants were evaluated, we exclusively report the results from quantized and deployed model versions, considering the range of worst- to best-performing models.
  4. For memory consumption, we systematically use model size, which corresponds to the flash memory needed to store model parameters on the MCU.

7.1 ARM-based MCUs

As discussed in Section 6.1.1, MCUs based on the ARM processor family are among the most widely used platforms across embedded computing, supporting a vast array of applications. Prior work has explored ARM-based MCU deployments in diverse domains such as object detection , speech processing , human activity recognition (HAR) , environmental monitoring , agriculture , healthcare , education , and network security .

Several works focus on privacy-preserving vision applications at the edge, including the egocentric privacy-preserving intelligent camera (EPIC) and XimSwap , which perform real-time video anonymization on smart cameras and edge-based vision sensors. 7.2 RISC-V-based MCUs

Similar to ARM-based systems, RISC-V-based MCUs are being considered across a growing range of application domains. As outlined in Section 6.1.2, the openness of RISC-V, which allows low-bit arithmetic, packed-SIMD instructions, dot-product units, and mixed-precision support to be introduced at the ISA and microarchitecture level, makes it well suited to quantized inference. To date, however, most of this potential has been realized in research platforms such as PULP and PULPissimo , in mixed-precision cores such as MPIC , and in NPU-augmented commercial SoCs such as GAP8 and GAP9, rather than in general-purpose silicon. The latter family, owing to its on-chip neural engines, accounts for the large majority of RISC-V application deployments and therefore belongs to the NPU-integrated category discussed in Section 7.3. This subsection instead focuses on commercial, general-purpose RISC-V MCUs such as the ESP32-C3, ESP32-C6, and ESP32-P4, which are inexpensive, widely available, and programmable with standard toolchains, making them the most likely targets for real-world quantized deployment outside research settings. These devices are positioned primarily as wireless IoT controllers rather than as dedicated inference platforms, and their software stack for quantized deployment remains less mature than the established ARM Cortex-M ecosystem.

As a result, only a few application deployments have so far been reported, covering healthcare and autonomous robotics . Rattanasak et al. combined knowledge distillation with INT8 post-training quantization to deploy a fetal-movement classifier on an ESP32-C6, compressing the model to 310 KB while retaining an 88.64% F1-score, and additionally reported on-device power, peaking at 179.8 mW. Industrial condition monitoring has been addressed with INT8 vibration-based fault classifiers by Banerjee et al. on an ESP32-C3 (a 0.95 macro-F1, an 80 KB model, and roughly 82 ms latency while consuming 6.77 mJ of energy per inference). The more capable dual-core ESP32-P4 has been used for heavier, vision-based workloads, as shown by TinyNav , which deploys an INT8 2D CNN for real-time ground-robot navigation, reaching 30 ms per inference and completing 40 collision-free laps in controlled track tests. Beyond these end applications, a parallel body of work targets the enabling toolchains and kernels. It includes benchmarking and on-device profiling frameworks together with memory-reduction and parallelization methods , evaluated on commercial RISC-V parts such as the ESP32-C3 and the SiFive FE310. Taken together, these deployments indicate that quantized inference on commercial RISC-V MCUs is feasible but still confined to the lower-to-middle tier of edge workloads. Compact one-dimensional and feature-based models, such as those for physiological-signal and vibration analysis, run comfortably on the single-core ESP32-C parts, whereas heavier two-dimensional perception becomes practical only on the dual-core ESP32-P4. The contrast with accelerator-integrated platforms is pronounced, since the small number of deployments collected here stands against the far more numerous GAP8 and GAP9 deployments surveyed in the following subsection, which their dedicated neural engines make possible. Echoing the assessment in Section 6.1.2, RISC-V is thus best regarded as a promising but still-maturing foundation for quantized inference, one whose application-level adoption currently rests on NPU-augmented designs rather than on general-purpose commercial MCUs.

Rashid and Mohsenin introduce TinyM2Net-V3, a memory-aware framework for compressed multimodal networks that combines knowledge distillation and low-bit quantization with memory-hierarchy-aware placement, fitting models into constrained on-chip memory while reducing latency and energy.

A further group of deployments targets resource-constrained robotics, motion estimation, and wearable healthcare. Rüegg et al. adapt KeyPointNet for MCU deployment in two variants, one minimizing model size and the other minimizing latency, and Büyüksolak and Güneş propose Tiny Visual Odometry (TinyVO), a real-time, low-power visual-odometry network intended to complement, or in some cases replace, global navigation satellite system (GNSS)- or inertial navigation system (INS)-based positioning. On the wearable side, Itani et al. build NeuralAids, a battery-powered hearing aid on the GAP9 that uses mixed-precision quantization and quantization-aware training to meet real-time speech-enhancement and power constraints, and Ingolfsson et al. present BioGAP, a GAP9-based EEG wearable for speech-imagery decoding that explores continual learning for user-specific adaptation.

The NPU-integrated deployments show the clearest latency and energy advantages of any family considered here , but also the strongest dependence on hardware-specific toolchains . Most reported systems still use INT8 even when the underlying accelerator advertises lower-precision capability, which suggests that practical adoption is limited by operator support, mapping constraints, memory partitioning, and deployment tooling rather than by arithmetic capability alone . For quantization research, this distinction matters, since accelerator-backed MCUs can make low-precision inference highly predictable, but only when the model architecture and quantization format are compatible with the accelerator’s supported execution path .

Taken together, these three hardware families reveal a consistent pattern. ARM-based MCUs offer the most established and portable deployment path and dominate when mature tooling and low-friction integration matter most . Commercial RISC-V MCUs show that quantized inference is feasible on a fully programmable open-ISA CPU, although their application base is smaller and more recent and their software stack less standardized . NPU-integrated MCUs, which include the clustered RISC-V GAP8 and GAP9 alongside dedicated CNN and operator accelerators, provide the strongest latency and energy predictability and now represent the leading edge of on-device inference, but only when the deployment flow is tightly matched to the accelerator. Across all three families, uniform INT8 remains the practical default , while mixed precision and more aggressive quantization appear only when the surrounding runtime and hardware stack can actually exploit them . These patterns motivate the cross-case recommendations that follow.

7.4 Cross-Case Recommendations

The surveyed applications show that deployment choices are best understood as workload-dependent trade-offs rather than as universally optimal hardware or quantization decisions. Three practical recommendations emerge.

1) Match the hardware to the workload. ARM Cortex-M remains the most practical baseline for modest-depth models and always-on sensing scenarios, where mature stacks such as TFLM or STM32Cube.AI reduce deployment effort and provide dependable performance in environmental monitoring, networking, and lightweight healthcare tasks . Commercial RISC-V MCUs provide a viable alternative for compact workloads, as the ESP32-C3, ESP32-C6, and ESP32-P4 deployments demonstrate . For higher-throughput workloads or applications that need stronger latency and energy predictability, NPU-integrated MCUs become attractive whenever the model is compatible with a vendor-aligned toolchain . Among these, the GAP family (GAP8 and GAP9) is particularly well-suited to reaction-critical and parallelizable perception loops, including drones, robotics, and fast human activity recognition, where clustered cores, DMA, tiling, and HWCE/NE16 support help amortize memory movement .

2) Let platform support guide the quantization strategy. Although the literature contains many advanced quantization strategies, most successful real deployments still rely on uniform INT8 through PTQ or QAT. This remains the best default because it offers the strongest balance between memory reduction, runtime simplicity, and accuracy retention. Mixed precision becomes most useful when profiling reveals clear layer sensitivity or when the runtime can exploit finer-grained precision control, as seen in selected GAP-family and adaptive ARM deployments . By contrast, extreme low-bit methods such as binary and ternary quantization remain more specialized and are most justifiable when flash memory or bandwidth is the dominant bottleneck .

3) Optimize the full deployment stack, not the model alone. The strongest application results rarely follow from quantization alone. Rather, they emerge when quantization is aligned with operator libraries, memory layout, compiler support, and hardware scheduling. Toolchain-aware deployment frameworks such as Autotiler, nntool, and ai8x-tools show that practical gains often depend as much on data movement and execution orchestration as on the nominal precision of the model itself . From an energy perspective, this also implies that deployment strategy should follow the operating profile of the task. Always-on systems benefit most from minimizing energy per useful inference, whereas episodic or safety-critical workloads may justify more conservative quantization in exchange for stronger robustness .

Overall, the application literature reinforces the central argument of this survey. On MCU-class devices, the outcome of quantized deployment is determined not by compression in isolation, but by the co-design of the model, the runtime, and the target hardware. 8 Challenges and Future Directions

Despite the progress surveyed here, several obstacles continue to limit the broader, more systematic deployment of quantized neural networks on MCU-class devices. These challenges are no longer purely algorithmic. Rather, they increasingly arise at the interfaces between quantization methods, deployment toolchains, runtime systems, and hardware support. We therefore present them in the order they arise in the deployment stack of Fig. 17, descending from the application to the silicon: operator and architecture coverage in the quantization toolchains; the numerical formats that the stack will not carry, from sub-8-bit integers to Posit; and the runtime and hardware limits that constrain mixed precision and on-device adaptation. The last two challenges, hardware-aware co-design and sustainability, span the full height of the stack rather than sitting between any two layers. The following subsections discuss these bottlenecks and outline the most promising directions for future work.

8.1 Operator Support in Quantization Toolchains

Current quantization frameworks often lack consistent operator coverage, leading to incompatibilities, especially when converting complete networks to their quantized forms. For instance, TensorFlow’s QAT pipeline requires manual annotations and replacements for many layer operations, reducing the portability of QAT-tuned models and complicating deployment on resource-constrained devices. Addressing this gap will require a unified operator support standard across libraries, supported by exporter–compiler co-design and validation tools that ensure full “quantizability” and provide safe replacements where native support is absent.

8.2 Extended Support for Emerging Architectures

Modern architectures, such as transformers and diffusion models, are gaining traction at the edge but remain largely unsupported in current quantization frameworks due to their non-convolutional operations and complex memory patterns.

Recent work shows that these models can be adapted for MCU-class hardware. Deeploy compiles quantized small language model inference for heterogeneous MCUs using static memory allocation, memory-aware tiling, and accelerator-aware code generation. Kaneko and Meng combine INT8 PTQ, row-buffer optimization, scale sharing, and SIMD kernels on the CV32E40p target, reporting a 263 KiB weight footprint and a 23% latency reduction from their integer implementation optimizations. Zheng addresses quantization challenges in diffusion models with optimizations for upsampling and denoising blocks, while Tiny Transformer illustrates structural modifications for biomedical tasks.

To fully integrate such architectures into embedded AI workflows, quantization toolchains must extend their support to non-CNN models, including those with attention-aware operators, advanced memory scheduling, and mixed precision flexibility. Recent advances form a foundation, but broader integration is needed to close this gap.

8.3 Limited Sub-8-Bit and QAT Support

Mainstream frameworks such as TensorFlow and PyTorch currently lack native support for quantization below 8 bits. As a result, researchers often turn to device-specific toolchains (e.g., MAX7800x frameworks) or custom implementations, which typically have limited documentation, non-standard workflows, and reduced reproducibility. This hinders adoption in adjacent applications and prevents building on standardized foundations. While 8-bit quantization remains the norm for MCU-class devices, more aggressive approaches see limited use primarily due to insufficient framework support.

Future work should extend popular frameworks to support sub-8-bit quantization, particularly for extreme edge devices, given that 4-bit and even binary models can be effective in many scenarios (see Section 4.2). Integrating QAT at these low precisions would further standardize workflows for latency- and energy-sensitive applications. Community-driven initiatives could also define a compact, quantization-aware model exchange format (e.g., an extension of ONNX) that encodes precision, scale, and operation-level metadata to promote interoperability and reduce engineering overhead.

8.4 Support for Alternative Numerical Formats

Conventional 8-bit and fixed-point formats limit flexibility across diverse edge workloads. Emerging formats like Posit offer improved dynamic range but lack mainstream hardware support. For example, POLARON introduces a trans-precision framework that adaptively switches between floating-point, fixed-point, and Posit representations. Its reconfigurable hardware and precision-aware controller enable runtime tuning based on workload sensitivity. Integrating trans-precision support into quantization toolchains and hardware platforms could enable more adaptive and efficient inference under tight resource constraints.

8.5 Static Memory Allocation Constraints and Mixed Precision

Most embedded frameworks, such as TFLM , rely on static memory allocation via a fixed tensor arena. While this simplifies management, it restricts the deployment of models with variable-shaped tensors or mixed-precision layers. Mixed precision quantization, which was shown to improve efficiency without compromising performance (see Section 4.3), is rarely supported due to this limitation, as hardware and toolchains are tied to fixed allocation schemes. RAMS addresses this limitation from the runtime side by scaling INT8 inference memory according to available resources and using cache-aware memory management. Compared with the widely used TFLM runtime, it reports up to a \(1.57\times\) speedup and a 67.74% reduction in power consumption with a comparable memory footprint. Such flexible runtimes remain uncommon in MCU deployment stacks.

To address these limitations, future work should explore dynamic memory virtualization at the software level and extend MCU-class deployment stacks to better support mixed-precision model parameters. This would be essential to support more complex models, flexible precision settings, and efficient multi-model deployment on memory-constrained hardware.

8.6 On-Device Training

While inference on MCUs has grown into a practical standard, enabling on-device training remains a major challenge due to tight memory, energy, and compute constraints. This limitation hinders applications that require continual learning, federated learning, or user-specific personalization. Several recent efforts have been made to address these challenges , yet adoption remains immature. TrainDeeploy explicitly extends Deeploy’s inference compiler with automatic differentiation and training-oriented passes while retaining its compile-time static allocation and tiling principles. For LoRA training, it reports a 23% reduction in dynamic memory use, \(15\times\) fewer trainable parameters and gradients, \(1.6\times\) less memory transfer, and \(2.3\)–\(3.5\times\) speedup from GEMM acceleration. However, the evaluation uses FP32 training on a simulated heterogeneous PULP-based SoC with a RedMulE accelerator, so it points to accelerator-assisted adaptation rather than training directly on a general-purpose MCU.

To enable on-device training, frameworks must support quantized gradient descent, efficient memory reallocation, and selective fine-tuning strategies. Although these components have been studied individually, future research can work on proposing robust frameworks for continual and federated learning directly on MCUs. This would extend MCU quantization beyond fixed offline inference to truly adaptive, privacy-preserving intelligence at the extreme edge.

8.7 Hardware-Aware Quantization Co-Design

A large body of quantization work continues to propose new algorithms, but methods that ignore hardware constraints often fail to deliver their expected benefits during deployment. This limitation is especially important for MCU-class devices, where memory capacity, operator support, execution kernels, and data movement strongly affect the realized latency, energy, and accuracy trade-offs. Co-optimizing quantization parameters with the underlying architecture can therefore help researchers better exploit limited memory and compute resources. This calls for more robust hardware-aware quantization and NAS methods tailored to specific MCU families. Although recent work has begun to explore this direction , further progress is needed on native hardware-aware optimization.

8.8 Sustainability and Environmental Impact

Although quantization is typically motivated by efficiency (latency, memory, and energy), its implications increasingly intersect with sustainability as TinyML systems scale. At the device level, lower precision can reduce operational energy by decreasing compute cost and, often more importantly, memory traffic, which in turn can extend battery lifetime and enable always-on sensing without frequent charging or replacement. However, sustainability arguments remain difficult to make rigorously because the energy footprint of an embedded ML application is rarely dominated by neural inference alone. Pre- and post-processing, sensor acquisition, and communication can materially shift the energy profile, meaning that model-only efficiency claims may not translate to system-level sustainability benefits. To put it simply, an embedded ML pipeline often includes substantial non-neural-network costs that must be accounted for when assessing its overall environmental impact.

Recent work has begun to explicitly position compression and low-bit quantization through a sustainability lens . However, to make sustainability a defensible and reproducible future direction for quantization on microcontrollers, we recommend that future work (i) treat sustainability as a first-class objective in quantization by explicitly optimizing accuracy–latency–memory alongside energy and, where feasible, carbon-aware metrics, (ii) report both end-to-end and phase-separated energy/latency so that quantization gains are not overstated or masked by pipeline overheads , (iii) evaluate system-level trade-offs jointly (compute versus communication, update frequency, and duty-cycle effects) rather than relying on model-only efficiency, and (iv) extend quantization toolchains to better support deployment regimes aligned with sustainability constraints, including lifetime-aware scheduling, energy-budgeted inference policies, and maintenance strategies that minimize costly re-training or frequent device-side updates.

The next phase of progress in MCU quantization will likely depend less on isolated improvements in low-bit arithmetic and more on the maturity of end-to-end deployment. Operator and architecture coverage in the toolchains must improve alongside the range of numerical formats the stack is willing to carry, and alongside the runtime and hardware flexibility that mixed precision and on-device adaptation demand. At the same time, hardware-aware co-design and system-level evaluation will be needed to ensure that quantization gains translate into measurable improvements in latency, energy, memory use, and sustainability. Future gains will therefore come from treating quantization as part of a unified deployment stack rather than as a standalone compression step, enabling MCU-scale intelligence that is efficient, robust, portable, and sustainable across real-world edge applications.

9 Conclusion

This survey provided a hardware-oriented, deployment-centered perspective on neural network quantization for MCU-class edge devices, synthesizing quantization methods, hardware platforms, software stacks, and deployment practices through the lens of practical MCU constraints. By systematically reviewing quantization techniques most relevant to MCUs and extreme-edge platforms, we highlighted the critical trade-offs among model accuracy, computational complexity, memory footprint, and numerical representations as hardware design factors, alongside accelerator support and memory hierarchies. From a deployment standpoint, we examined the three major families of MCU platforms, namely ARM-based, RISC-V-based, and NPU-integrated designs, together with the software stacks actively used in the literature to enable TinyML. We further consolidated real-world applications and practitioner-oriented insights across domains such as audio, vision, healthcare, robotics, and environmental sensing, and identified persistent challenges that shape current and future MCU deployments, including the role of quantization as a potential enabler of sustainable AI when evaluated at the system level.

Several cross-cutting patterns emerge. Firstly, INT8 quantization (both PTQ and QAT) remains the practical go-to for edge deployment, with the most durable gains coming from per-channel weight quantization, bias correction, cross-layer equalization, and quantization-aware fine-tuning. Sub-8-bit precision mainly helps under tight energy budgets, especially on architectures that support extremely low bit-widths. We also observe a shift in priorities across application domains, with healthcare leaning towards accuracy and reliability due to its safety-sensitive nature, whilst HAR and drones prioritize end-to-end latency and energy consumption. Reflecting these priorities, deployments are most consistent and reproducible on NPU-integrated MCUs (e.g., Ethos-U/STM32N6, GAP8/9, MAX78000/78002) when paired with their vendor toolchains, which constrain operator sets and tensor layouts, allocate static memory arenas, and target low-bit inference, making the latency, energy, and accuracy trade-offs more predictable.

At the framework and runtime level, toolchains are maturing, but important gaps remain. For example, TFLM’s static arenas, uneven operator coverage, and limited sub-8-bit support complicate portability and mixed-precision execution. With the rise of PyTorch-edge solutions (e.g., ExecuTorch), PyTorch-centered deployment flows are likely to become more common in MCU-oriented workflows, while device-specific flows (e.g., ai8x-training/ai8x-synthesis) show the continued importance of tightly integrated quantization, mapping, and code generation. Other emerging directions, such as dynamic memory virtualization and exporter–compiler co-design, point to a healthier path where accuracy, latency, energy, and memory are co-optimized rather than traded off.

Looking ahead, the most important advances are likely to come from improving the deployment stack around quantization rather than from reducing precision alone. Broader sub-8-bit and mixed-precision support will be valuable only when matched by reliable operator coverage, dynamic memory handling, accelerator-aware compilation, and hardware backends that can efficiently exploit these formats. Similarly, on-device training, continual learning, and federated learning will require quantized update mechanisms that are compatible with MCU memory, energy, and runtime constraints. Beyond model-level efficiency, future work should increasingly treat sustainability as a system-level objective by jointly optimizing accuracy, latency, memory, and energy, and by evaluating quantization benefits using end-to-end measurements. Ultimately, quantization for MCUs should be treated not as a standalone compression step, but as part of a unified hardware–software deployment stack for reliable, low-latency, and environmentally sustainable intelligence at the extreme edge.
